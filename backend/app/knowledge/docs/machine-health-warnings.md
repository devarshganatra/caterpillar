## Overview

Engine temperature and hydraulic pressure thresholds exist because both
systems have a point past which continued operation risks real damage, not
just reduced performance. A threshold warning is a hard limit, not an
early-caution zone with room to negotiate.

## Engine Temperature

Sustained high engine temperature accelerates wear on seals and gaskets
and, past a certain point, risks warping components that are expensive and
slow to replace. A single brief spike during heavy sustained load can be
normal; a temperature that keeps climbing rather than stabilizing (a
persistent positive slope) is the more concerning pattern, since it
suggests the cooling system is not keeping pace with heat generation.

## Hydraulic Pressure

Hydraulic systems are designed with a working pressure envelope; operating
above it stresses hoses, seals, and pump components, and meaningfully
raises the chance of a hose failure — which is both a maintenance cost and
a safety hazard from a high-pressure fluid injection injury. Pressure
warnings should not be treated as "the machine being cautious."

## Why This Attributes Idle Time To The Machine, Not The Operator

When a health threshold is active, any idle time that follows is
reasonably attributed to the machine, not the operator: continuing to work
through an active fault condition is the wrong response, and stopping is
the correct one. This attribution protects operators from being penalized
for correctly taking a machine out of service.

## Recommended Response

Stop the current operation, allow the machine to idle down if temperature
is the concern, and report the warning for a maintenance check before
resuming full-load work. Do not reset or ignore a repeated threshold
warning without an inspection.
