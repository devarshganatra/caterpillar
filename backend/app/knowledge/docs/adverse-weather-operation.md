## Overview

Rain, high wind, and poor visibility change how a machine should be
operated, not just how it feels to operate. Traction, stopping distance,
and an operator's reaction time all degrade together in bad weather, which
is why speed caps and safety-zone radii are tied to weather conditions
rather than left fixed.

## Rain And Ground Condition

Heavy rain both reduces visibility and, over time, saturates the ground
enough to change it from dry to wet to muddy. Muddy ground reduces track
and tire grip, increasing stopping distance and the risk of an
uncontrolled slide on a grade. Travel speed caps should be treated as a
hard limit in these conditions, not a suggestion.

## Wind And Boom Work

High wind primarily affects high-boom or high-load operations, where wind
load on a raised boom or suspended load can affect machine stability.
Operators should be more conservative with boom height and swing speed
when wind exceeds the caution threshold for their machine class.

## Why Task Duration Increases In Bad Weather

A task that normally takes 40 minutes in clear conditions genuinely takes
longer in rain or mud: cycle times slow down because of reduced speed
caps, and machines may need to reposition more carefully. An ETA estimate
that accounts for weather is not being pessimistic — it reflects real,
measured slowdown. If the live ETA is slipping in bad weather, this is
usually the primary cause worth checking first, before looking at
operator-caused idle time.

## Recommended Response

Confirm the active safety envelope shown in the cab reflects current
conditions before starting work, and treat a widened zone or lowered speed
cap as the new normal for that shift segment, not a temporary annoyance to
work around.
